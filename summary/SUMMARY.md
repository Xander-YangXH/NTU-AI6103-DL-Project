## 2. Findings

1. **GW-EVA does not beat EVA (H2 not supported).** Under the shared protocol G5 − G3 is −0.15, −0.88, −1.23 and −0.46 points on SST-2, MRPC, RTE and low resource; every interval contains 0 and every mean is slightly negative. Only the step-0 part of H2 holds: GW-EVA's step-0 gradient on B is 1.3–2.1× EVA's (e.g. 3.36 vs 1.63 on MRPC). H2's other early-training claim, a lower loss over the first 500 steps, fails at equal learning rates: G5 and G3 are indistinguishable in steps 1–50 on every task, and on SST-2 G5's loss is slightly higher afterwards (+0.02, significant).
2. **On the full-data tasks, no initialisation beats plain LoRA at each group's tuned learning rate.** With the shared five seeds, all of G2–G6, G8 and G9 are within ±1.9 points of G1 on every task, and every interval contains 0. The spread between the best and worst of these eight groups is under 1.2 points on SST-2 and MRPC, smaller than the seed noise on RTE. The apparent MRPC edge of EVA with ten seeds (+0.57) does not survive a same-GPU check: rerun on L40S, G3's seeds 0–4 score 1.08 lower than on A40, and with 15 seeds all on L40S G3 − G1 = +0.14 [−0.24, +0.51]. On RTE, EVA does help when the learning rate is held fixed: at lr 2e-4, G3 − G1 = +2.13 [+0.97, +3.29] over 10 seeds (+3.03 on the same-GPU seeds alone). G1's own tuned lr (5e-4) closes most of that gap, at the price of an occasional collapse (1 of 10 seeds), so at each group's tuned lr the difference is not significant.
3. **Data-aware initialisations fit faster early on, but the advantage does not last.** On MRPC, where all groups share lr 2e-4, EVA, GW-EVA and LoRA-GA all have a significantly lower training loss than plain LoRA throughout the first 500 steps (−0.014 to −0.024 in steps 1–50, about −0.055 in steps 51–200), yet their final F1 is not significantly different. Matching only the magnitude (G2) moves the loss by 0.004 at most.
4. **A larger initial gradient does not translate into accuracy.** The step-0 gradient grows 7–37× from G1 to G3–G7. Matching EVA's row norm with random directions (G2) changes nothing; EVA's directions (G3) add a 4–9× larger gradient at the same norm, GW-EVA (G5) another 1.3–2.1×, and accuracy stays flat throughout. G8 even has a 1.4–1.7× larger step-0 gradient than G3 on MRPC and RTE while being slightly less accurate. The magnitude part follows the theory: the measured G2 / G1 ratio is 1.78–1.83 against the predicted √3 ≈ 1.73, so the gradient identity holds and the larger gradients are real; they just do not matter for the final accuracy.
5. **With little data, EVA is worse than plain LoRA only because of the learning rate.** Under the shared rule (low resource reuses the full-SST-2 learning rate) G3 trails G1 by 2.64 points (−2.58 with ten seeds). G1 is insensitive to the learning rate (2e-4, 5e-4 and 1e-3 all give 91.4–91.5). EVA is not: lowering G3's learning rate from 1e-3 to 5e-4 and 2e-4 raises it from 89.50 to 90.55 and 90.87 (same GPU), so the shared rule gave EVA its worst setting. With both at 2e-4 on the same GPU, G3 − G1 = −0.57 [−1.45, +0.30], not significant. Keeping EVA's directions but removing its rank redistribution (ρ = 1, exact budget) changes nothing (−0.41 [−2.32, +1.50]). The loss curves at lr 1e-3 show what goes wrong at the high learning rate: EVA and GW-EVA start level with LoRA and then fit the training set more slowly in steps 51–200 (+0.09 and +0.15, significant), an optimisation problem rather than overfitting. Two earlier statements were wrong: that the gap "is not a learning-rate effect" (the G1 control only showed that G1 does not depend on it), and that a significant gap of 0.57 remains at EVA's best learning rate (that compared G3 at 2e-4 with G1 at 5e-4). G8 (EVA on generic text) gains only 0.4–0.5 points at lower learning rates and stays about 1 point below G1 (−1.03 [−2.28, +0.22] at equal lr 2e-4).
6. **Allocating ranks by parameter cost (H3) has no effect on full data.** G4 − G3 and G6 − G5 are all non-significant on SST-2, MRPC and RTE. The one significant difference, G6 − G5 = +1.53 on low resource, is most likely the learning rate (footnote 2), and ρ = 1 vs ρ = 2 for EVA in low resource shows no allocation effect either.
7. **RTE training is fragile, and LoRA-GA (G7) is the worst case.** G7 averages 56.68 on RTE, 17.3 points below G1 and significant: seed 1 predicts one class for all 277 examples and seeds 2–3 are near chance; on SST-2 and MRPC it matches the others. Single-class collapse is not unique to LoRA-GA, though: with ten seeds, plain LoRA (G1, lr 5e-4) collapses on seed 8 (52.71, training loss stuck at ln 2), and at lr 1e-3 six of the seven tuned groups collapse to 48% on the RTE dev split. G7 differs in degree, with all five seeds below 67 against nine of ten above 70 for G1. RTE results should therefore be reported with the collapse rate, not just the mean.
8. **Where the calibration data comes from barely matters (H4).** EVA calibrated on another task (G9) or on generic wikitext-103 text (G8) is never significantly better or worse than plain LoRA on full data. Against EVA calibrated on the task itself (G3), the only significant difference is G8 on MRPC (−0.91 [−1.54, −0.28], with G8's small variance and no correction for the ~20 comparisons made here), where G8 also fits slightly more slowly early on (+0.007 to +0.015 loss). The subspaces themselves differ much more: G8 overlaps G3 by 0.64–0.72 and G9 by 0.49–0.66, while EVA calibrated on a disjoint sample of the same task overlaps G3 by 0.92–0.95 (random subspaces: 0.01). So the calibration source genuinely moves a quarter to half of the subspace beyond sampling noise. G8, which keeps G3's token layout and only swaps the words, overlaps more than G9, which changes both the task and the format; most of the difference is on SST-2, where G9's calibration switches from single sentences to sentence pairs, so part of what looked like task content may be input format. Together with findings 1–4, this suggests that at r = 8 on RoBERTa-base, accuracy is insensitive to which directions A starts from.
9. **The GPU shifts single runs, and sometimes means.** Rerunning G3 with the same code, versions and seeds on L40S instead of A40 changes single low-resource seeds by up to 2.5 points (the first training-step loss already differs, from bf16 numerics in the head warm-up), and moves MRPC down by 1.08 on average (−2.43 to +0.27). Cross-GPU seed pairing is therefore unreliable: it produced the MRPC "edge" of finding 2, and G8's +1.08 over G3 in low resource shrinks to +0.39 against the same-GPU rerun. Comparisons that decide a finding should use runs from one GPU.
10. **GW-EVA's subspace really differs from EVA's (H1).** On the same 1,024 calibration examples, the top-8 subspaces of Σ_g and of EVA's centred activation covariance overlap 0.64 on SST-2, 0.52 on MRPC, 0.49 on RTE and 0.69 on low-resource SST-2 (mean principal angle 31–46°). GW-EVA calibrated on two disjoint samples of the same task overlaps itself 0.84–0.95. The gap to that floor, −0.20 to −0.37, is significant on every task, and only 7 of 864 layer-seed pairs reach the floor. It is spread over all layer types (attention output o lowest at 0.48, k and v highest at 0.61) and depths. Per token, ‖g‖² is only weakly correlated with ‖x‖² (Pearson 0.10–0.19 on average), so the weighting is not a rescaled activation norm. GW-EVA thus moves the subspace about as far as calibrating EVA on other data does (finding 8), and, as there, accuracy does not follow (finding 1). Caveats: the EVA side is centred and the GW side is not, so centring and gradient weighting are not separated; this run has a GW–GW floor but no EVA–EVA one (H4's EVA floor, 0.92–0.95, comes from another script at the groups' own ranks); the low-resource floor samples come from full SST-2 outside the 1k subset.

### Hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| H1: Σ_g's subspace differs from the activation covariance's | **Supported** | Top-8 overlap with EVA 0.49–0.69 against a GW–GW floor of 0.84–0.95, significant on all four tasks; ‖g‖² only weakly correlated with ‖x‖² (Pearson 0.10–0.19) (finding 10) |
| H2: GW-EVA has a larger step-0 gradient, lower early loss and higher accuracy than EVA | **Not supported** | Only the step-0 gradient is larger; early loss equal or slightly higher, accuracy non-significant with slightly negative means (finding 1) |
| H3: cost-based allocation changes the result | **Not supported** | G4 − G3, G6 − G5 non-significant on full data; the low-resource G6 − G5 is most likely learning rate, and EVA with uniform ranks matches ρ = 2 (findings 5, 6) |
| H4: how task-specific the inputs are | **Answered** | The subspace is task-specific (0.49–0.72 against a same-task floor of 0.92–0.95), but task data, another task and generic text give the same accuracy; part of the difference may be input format rather than content (finding 8) |

Following the decision table in [`NEXT_STEPS.md`](NEXT_STEPS.md), the results point to the **analysis / negative-result** story: data-aware LoRA initialisations (EVA, GW-EVA, LoRA-GA) change the early gradients a lot and speed up early fitting, but at a tuned learning rate leave the final accuracy unchanged on these tasks; in low resource they need a lower learning rate than LoRA and then match it. The one gain, EVA on RTE at a fixed learning rate, is a robustness effect (LoRA needs a higher learning rate there, which occasionally collapses) rather than higher attainable accuracy. H1 settles the branch: the subspaces differ (finding 10), so this is the "subspaces differ, no accuracy difference" row of the table. The gradient-weighted subspace is genuinely different from EVA's, by about as much as EVA calibrated on other data, and neither difference changes the final accuracy.
